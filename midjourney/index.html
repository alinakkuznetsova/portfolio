From Hand to Algorithm

I took one of my oil paintings and directed Midjourney to turn it into two products, a chrome candle holder and a ceramic vase. The question was how much of a painting's anatomy and brushwork can survive being handed to a generative model, and what it takes to control that.

Medium
Oil painting into AI product concepts
Tool
Midjourney V7
Controls
Image weight, style reference, prompt
Outputs
2 product concepts, 9 documented iterations
A.1The concept

Start from something I made, not from a blank prompt.

I paint in oil, and I had been learning Midjourney through courses. Instead of prompting from nothing, I wanted to put my own work at the centre of the process: use a skull I had painted as the reference, and ask the model to translate its texture, anatomy and mood into real, makeable objects.

Why a skullA skull already suggests a vessel, and the painting's near-monochrome palette carries over naturally to chrome and ceramic.

The test was whether the weight of the brushwork and the particular anatomy I had studied would come through in a product render, or be smoothed away. Everything that follows is about controlling that.

A.2Into product

Two objects that still read as the painting.

Product IFinal

Chrome skull candle holder

The cranial dome, deep eye sockets and jaw all carry through from the painting. The chrome keeps a faint paint-like texture instead of reading as perfectly smooth, an echo of the brushwork.

hyperrealistic chrome skull candle holder, top of skull removed with white wax candle inside with wick, slight overhead, front-on angle, professional studio product lighting, plain white background, commercial product photography, shot on 85mm lens --chaos 5 --raw --stylize 50 --iw 0.7

Product IIFinal

Ceramic skull vase

The proportions, eye cavities and irregular bone structure follow the painting closely. A matte glaze with slight roughness nods to the paint surface rather than going glossy.

ceramic skull vase, dried wildflowers and botanicals emerging from open cranium, dusty rose, ivory, sage, muted neutral tones, warm sandy beige background, dramatic natural side lighting, long soft shadow cast on wall, editorial product photography --raw --sw 50 --iw 0.5

A.3Process

Every product took a run of useful failures.

These are the documented steps behind each product: what I changed, what went wrong, and the exact prompt and parameters each time.

    No reference, no texture

    Without a reference the flowers correctly grew from inside, like a vase. But it was glossy with no paint texture, and looked nothing like my painting.

  2. Iteration 2

    The references overrode the prompt

    I accidentally put my painting in both the image and style reference slots, and the output came back as a painting rather than a vase. The prompt was right; the inputs won. A lesson in how much references control the result.

    Output is a painting

    Not quite a vase

    A simple prompt to see what needed changing. The flowers were stuck into the ceramic from outside, but the anatomy and paint-like texture were getting close. Progress on material, not yet on product logic.

    A new style reference, a different object

    I added a style reference to help the model understand the vase form. This one pushed it into an abstract, sculptural result: interesting on its own terms, but with none of my painting's anatomy or texture.

A.4What I learnt

Controlling a generative model is mostly about inputs.

Prompting

Less is more

Overloaded prompts confused the model and produced unwanted results. Simpler, clearer language consistently did better.

Image weight

--iw decides everything

Too high and the output stops reading as a product; too low and the texture and anatomy disappear. Finding the balance was the key to the translation.

References

The wrong reference wins

A style reference can pull the output into abstraction or bring it right to the brief. Choosing it deserves as much care as the prompt.

Iteration

Patience is a technique

The finals took far more attempts than are shown here. A willingness to keep testing mattered as much as any prompt trick.
